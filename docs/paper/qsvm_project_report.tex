\title{QTagger+ --- QSVM Track\\[0.3em]
\large Fidelity-kernel QSVM against classical baselines\\for malware family attribution}
\date{2 August 2026}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
QTagger+ compares classical machine-learning baselines against a fidelity-kernel
quantum support-vector machine (QSVM) on malware classification, under a
protocol designed so that the two are measured identically. It reports the
QSVM track in full.

Two datasets are used, each with a binary and a 15-class task. On the binary
task of CIC-MalMem every method exceeds $0.9998$ macro-F1, so that task cannot
rank methods and is treated as a control rather than a result. On the three
configurations where the task discriminates, classical models beat the QSVM
with $p<0.05$ on all 84 paired comparisons. The QSVM's failure on CIC 15-class is a property of the kernel
rather than the data --- a classical RBF kernel on the identical features does
not concentrate, while the fidelity kernel collapses to $0.40\times$ its spread.
\end{abstract}

\section{Classical baselines, and the saturation problem}

The starting point is four classical models --- random forest, XGBoost,
LightGBM, and an SVM with an RBF kernel --- tuned and evaluated on the full
CIC-MalMem-2022 dataset: \num{58596} rows, 55 memory-forensic features, 5-fold
cross-validation with an Optuna inner loop.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{\HFIG{fig_w1_baseline.pdf}}
\caption{Full-dataset CIC-MalMem, no dimensionality reduction. (a) The binary
task is saturated --- all four models exceed $0.9998$ macro-F1 and the spread
between best and worst is $10^{-4}$. (b) The multiclass task in its original
form, benign still included as a class, separates the models by 0.19 macro-F1.
These full-dataset runs predate experiment tracking in this project and are
transcribed from its written record.}
\label{fig:w1}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:w1}(a) shapes everything that follows. When every model scores
above $0.9998$, the task cannot rank methods: any comparison on it measures
noise. The project's central claims are therefore made on the multiclass task,
and a level result on CIC binary --- which does occur --- is read as a property
of the task rather than evidence of parity.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.46\textwidth]{\HFIG{fig_w1_cost.pdf}}
\caption{Tuning cost for a single outer fold on the full dataset. The SVM ---
the closest classical analogue to a QSVM, and therefore the most relevant
comparison --- is the most expensive at 72 minutes per fold.}
\label{fig:w1cost}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:w1cost} sets a constraint that runs through the whole project:
even the classical side is expensive at full scale, and the quantum side is
quadratically worse. The subsampling design in §\ref{sec:cost} follows from it.

\section{The comparison protocol}

\subsection{What makes the comparison fair}

Both frameworks consume the output of one shared pipeline: a zero-variance
filter, a correlation filter at $0.95$, standardisation, then PCA to \nc{}
components. That single parameter fixes the classical feature count \emph{and}
the quantum qubit budget simultaneously. The pipeline is fit on training-fold
rows only.

Because the fidelity kernel needs $O(n^2)$ circuit evaluations, quantum runs
work on a stratified 1000-row subsample. The subsample and the five outer folds
computed over it are written to disk by the quantum runner and \emph{replayed}
by the classical runner, so neither side chooses its own rows. A full-dataset
classical run is never compared against a subsampled quantum run.

\subsection{The cost that forces subsampling}
\label{sec:cost}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.52\textwidth]{\HFIG{fig_quantum_cost.pdf}}
\caption{Per-circuit-pair cost of building the training Gram matrix, against
qubit count, from a single session so the worker configuration is constant.
Cost per pair rises roughly tenfold from 1 to 8 qubits --- and the number of
pairs itself grows as $n^2$, so the two effects compound.}
\label{fig:qcost}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:qcost} is measured per circuit pair rather than per fold, which
removes the effect of subsample size changing across the project. At $n=1000$ a
training fold needs roughly \num{320000} pair evaluations, so the per-pair
figures translate to minutes per Gram at the low end and tens of minutes at
$n_{\mathrm{qubits}}=8$.

A sizing exercise fitted this scaling law and solved it for a target budget,
recommending $n=900$ at 60\,s per Gram build, with a class-balance floor of
$n \ge 750$ for the 15-class task. The 1000-row subsample used throughout sits
just above that floor.

\subsection{Binary is a ceiling, on the subsample too}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{\HFIG{fig_cic_binary_ceiling.pdf}}
\caption{CIC-MalMem binary on the 1000-row subsample, across the PCA sweep.
Every model, quantum and classical, lies between $0.977$ and $0.992$ macro-F1,
and every difference is inside the fold-to-fold error bars. Aggregated over all
recorded folds: these runs predate parent/child run tracking, so individual

\label{fig:ceiling}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:ceiling} shows that the saturation of Figure~\ref{fig:w1}(a)
survives dimensionality reduction and subsampling. The QSVM does sit level with
the classical models here. It sits level with them because nothing separates on
this task, not because the quantum kernel is competitive.

\subsection{The multiclass reframe}

The original multiclass task kept benign as a 16th class. Since benign detection
is exactly what the binary task measures, keeping it made the label space
pathologically imbalanced and duplicated the binary task inside the multiclass
one. The task was reframed: benign rows are dropped, leaving \num{29298}
malware rows across 15 families. All 15-class numbers in this report use that
definition, which is why they are not comparable to Figure~\ref{fig:w1}(b).

\section{A second dataset, and the encoding comparison}

\subsection{EMBER 2018}

A second dataset tests whether any conclusion is an artifact of CIC. EMBER 2018 contributes static PE features in 2381 dimensions. Work uses an
\num{18014}-row extract of the published test set; the full set is a single
$200\,000 \times 2384$ block that cannot be read incrementally. Its 15-class task
keeps the 15 largest families and downsamples each to the smallest, giving
\num{14325} rows at exactly 955 per family --- a balanced counterpart to CIC's
$1.7\times$ imbalance.

\subsection{angle versus IQP}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{\HFIG{fig_encoding_verdict.pdf}}
\caption{IQP minus angle encoding, 15-class macro-F1. The grey band is the
fold-to-fold spread of the encodings themselves. On CIC every difference lies
inside that band. On EMBER the differences at \nc{}~$=3$ and $6$ clear it.}
\label{fig:enc}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:enc} gives the verdict: IQP is never worse than angle, but the
advantage is only measurable on EMBER. On CIC the deltas are $+0.0009$,
$+0.0122$, $+0.0113$ and $+0.0046$ at \nc{} $=1,3,6,8$, each smaller than either
encoding's own standard deviation. Extending to 8 qubits did not rescue CIC
15-class performance ($0.0739$, against a $0.067$ chance baseline), which ruled
out qubit count as the missing ingredient.

A methodological change came out of this: comparing encodings by counting which
one an inner-CV tuner selected per fold was retired. It conflates ``which was
picked when both were live options'' with ``which performs better'', and
produced an ambiguous 8/15 win count on data where the direct macro-F1 deltas
showed IQP ahead at every \nc{}. Encoding comparisons now use dedicated
single-encoding runs and report deltas with their spread.

\subsection{SOREL-20M}

A third dataset was scoped and its multiclass labelling scheme designed and
validated over 19.7M rows: dominant-tag argmax over 11 behaviour-tag counts,
ties broken by declared column order, all-zero-tag rows dropped. The feature
store was never fetched and no sweep was run --- these are independent pieces of
work and only the first was completed. §\ref{sec:notdone} states the blocker.

\subsection{The subsample the project rests on}

Every quantum result is computed on 1000 rows, so the subsample must represent
its population. Read against the null expectation --- a faithful subsample still rejects
about $\alpha \cdot n_{\mathrm{features}}$ features by chance --- CIC rejects
\textbf{0} of 55 where $\approx 2.75$ were expected, and EMBER \textbf{1} of
2381 where $\approx 119$ were. Maximum class-proportion drift is below $0.001$
on both. Both subsamples are faithful.

\subsection{Why CIC 15-class collapses}

The QSVM sits at the random baseline on CIC 15-class ($0.056$--$0.079$ against
chance $\approx 0.067$) while reaching $0.42$--$0.52$ on EMBER 15-class. Class
imbalance and qubit count are both ruled out --- EMBER's families are exactly
balanced and CIC's only $1.7\times$ imbalanced, and extending CIC to 8 qubits
does not help. Two further measurements separate a dataset effect from a kernel
effect.

\paragraph{Feature geometry --- a dataset effect.} On the identical projected
features, EMBER's families are $\approx 8\times$ more separable by Fisher ratio
and up to $50\times$ on the worst-case family. CIC's silhouette is negative at
every \nc{}: the average point lies closer to another family's centroid than its
own. CIC's PCA nonetheless explains \emph{more} variance ($33$--$81\%$ against
$8$--$30\%$), so its variance sits in directions that do not discriminate family.
This explains why every method does badly on CIC 15-class, and cannot explain
the QSVM-specific failure --- a classical SVM extracts $0.108$--$0.165$ from
these same features where the QSVM extracts $0.056$--$0.079$.

\paragraph{Kernel geometry --- a kernel effect.}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{\WFIG{fig_kernel_diagnostics.pdf}}
\caption{First outer training fold, \nc{}~$=6$, IQP encoding, 800 rows, with an
RBF control built on the \emph{identical} feature matrix. (a) Off-diagonal Gram
spread. (b) Kernel-target alignment relative to its floor --- a constant kernel
scores $1/\sqrt{15}\approx 0.258$ on 15 balanced classes, so only the excess
carries information.}
\label{fig:kernel}
\end{figure}

The RBF control produces near-identical spread on both datasets ($0.2212$ against
$0.2184$), so CIC's features do not make a classical kernel concentrate. The
fidelity kernel tracks RBF on EMBER ($0.92\times$) and collapses to
$\mathbf{0.40\times}$ on CIC: every off-diagonal similarity squeezed into a band
$2.5\times$ narrower from the same inputs. The Gram approaches a scaled identity,
and a support-vector machine given that has nothing to separate on.

On alignment, the CIC fidelity kernel lands $0.000069$ \emph{below} the
constant-kernel floor --- indistinguishable from an all-ones matrix --- and the
RBF control is below it too. On EMBER both clear it by $\approx 0.03$. So
alignment separates the datasets and concentration separates the kernels.

Two limits on this. First, \texttt{bandwidth} is the named mechanism for
concentration and has never been tuned on CIC beyond its default; no sweep was
run, so this is a hypothesis with an identified lever rather than a measured
fix. Second, alignment reports that neither kernel carries label information on
CIC, yet a classical SVM reaches $0.16$ there --- alignment is a global
unweighted average over all pairs and an SVM requires no such thing, which makes
it a poor predictor of achievable SVM performance.

\subsection{Significance}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.56\textwidth]{\WFIG{fig_significance.pdf}}
\caption{All 84 testable quantum--classical pairs. Every point lies left of
$p=0.05$ and above zero.}
\label{fig:sig}
\end{figure}

All 84 testable pairs favour the classical model and all 84 reach $p<0.05$ on
the paired $t$-test; the worst $p$-value across every pair is $0.0241$.

The Wilcoxon signed-rank test returned exactly $0.0625$ for all 84 pairs. With
five paired samples that is the smallest attainable two-sided $p$-value, so the
test cannot reach $0.05$ at this fold count regardless of effect size; all 84
hitting the floor means every pair achieved the most extreme rank configuration
available. Six folds would put $0.03125$ within reach.

\section{What was not done}
\label{sec:notdone}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2em,style=nextline]
\item[SOREL-20M sweeps] Closed rather than deferred. Neither disk
(\SI{227}{\gibi\byte} free against \SI{71.6}{\gibi\byte} needed) nor memory is
the blocker --- the feature store is a memory-mapped LMDB whose reads page
through the OS cache. The blocker is the fixed \SI{71.6}{\gibi\byte} transfer:
the store offers no key-level remote access, so there is no way to fetch only the
thousand rows a sweep would need. Resume conditions are documented.
\item[Variational quantum classifier] Not implemented. The comparison is
fidelity-kernel QSVM against classical baselines, not ``quantum methods''
against classical ones.
\item[Basis encoding] Deliberately omitted. It maps discrete values onto
computational basis states, but the pipeline's inputs are continuous PCA
components, so it would require a quantisation step that discards what PCA was
used to retain.
\item[UMAP as a second reduction] Deliberately omitted. PCA is the
classical/quantum alignment point; a second reduction with no classical
counterpart would break it.
\item[CTGAN-augmented run] Blocked on the augmented dataset from another team.
The pipeline accepts any parquet, so nothing is outstanding on this side.
\item[Weighted F1] Implemented, but no sweep was re-run afterwards, so no
result in this report carries it. It matters most on CIC 15-class, where
families run 48--82 rows in the subsample.
\end{description}

\section{Untestable configurations}
\label{sec:untestable}

All CIC binary comparisons --- 9 cells across 4 models --- cannot be
significance-tested. Every CIC binary run, classical and quantum alike, predates
parent/child run tracking in the experiment log and carries no recoverable sweep
boundary; the 45 fold records per model resolve into two distinct sweeps per
\nc{} with different configurations and no identifier separating them. The
records are valid measurements and are used descriptively in
Figures~\ref{fig:ceiling} and~\ref{fig:arc}; no grouping was reconstructed,
since the time gaps between runs are not a recorded boundary. The limitation is
symmetric across frameworks.

McNemar's test between frameworks needs per-sample predictions from both models
on a shared test set. QSVM predictions were not persisted during these sweeps;
persistence was added afterwards but no sweep was re-run.

\section{Method notes}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Macro-F1, not accuracy.} On EMBER binary at \nc{}~$=1$ the QSVM
attains $0.890$ accuracy in three of five folds while the benign class's F1 is
exactly $0.000$ in those same folds, because $88.9\%$ of rows are malware and
the model labels everything malware.
\item \textbf{Runs are grouped by sweep, not by parameters.} The same
configuration was swept several times across the project, so a parameter-based
grouping returns 10, 15 or 20 records for a five-fold sweep and averages
distinct hyperparameter searches together --- 57 parameter groups in the
experiment log have a size other than five. For the QSVM, encoding is
additionally a \emph{per-fold outcome} rather than a configuration key: the
two-tier tuner selects it inside each fold, so one joint sweep can appear as
four angle folds and one IQP fold. Runs are grouped by parent run, keeping only
sweeps that completed with exactly five folds. 123 of 125 nested sweeps are
clean.
\item \textbf{Simulator only.} All results use a state-vector simulator. No
hardware execution and no noise model.
\end{itemize}

\section{Summary}

Across two datasets and two tasks, classical models beat the fidelity-kernel
QSVM on every configuration where the task can discriminate at all, with
$p<0.05$ on all 84 paired comparisons. The single configuration where the two
were level --- CIC binary --- is saturated for every method, and level results
there carry no information about the methods.

The transferable finding is the mechanism rather than the ranking. The fidelity
kernel does not fail on CIC because CIC's features are hard: a classical RBF
kernel on identical features does not concentrate. It fails because the fidelity
kernel itself collapses to $0.40\times$ the classical kernel's spread on that
data, driving its Gram matrix toward a scaled identity. That is a statement about
the method, and it predicts where the approach will struggle on data nobody in
this project has seen.

\paragraph{Next steps.} Tune \texttt{bandwidth} on CIC, the identified mechanism
whose lever remains untested. Move to six outer folds so a non-parametric test
becomes usable.
