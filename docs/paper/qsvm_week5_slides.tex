\begin{frame}{Setup --- what makes the comparison fair}
\small
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
\textbf{CIC-MalMem-2022} --- memory dumps, 55 forensic features
\begin{itemize}\small
  \item binary: \num{58596} rows, balanced
  \item 15-class: \num{29298} malware rows, $1.7\times$ imbalanced
\end{itemize}
\vspace{0.4em}
\textbf{EMBER 2018} --- static PE, 2381 features
\begin{itemize}\small
  \item binary: \num{18014} rows, $88.9\%$ malware
  \item 15-class: \num{14325} rows, 955 per family
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
\textbf{Protocol}
\begin{itemize}\small
  \item quantum kernel is $O(n^2)$ $\Rightarrow$ stratified 1000-row subsample
  \item subsample + 5 folds \emph{persisted, then replayed} by the classical
        runner --- identical rows, identical folds
  \item one shared pipeline ending in PCA to \nc{}
  \item \nc{} fixes classical feature count \emph{and} qubit budget at once
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}

\vspace{0.3em}
\footnotesize
\textbf{Metric is macro-F1, not accuracy.} EMBER binary at \nc{}~$=1$: the QSVM
reaches $0.890$ accuracy in 3 of 5 folds while benign-class F1 is exactly
$0.000$ --- $88.9\%$ of rows are malware and it labels everything malware.
\end{frame}
\note{
\textbf{Setup (60s). Skip fast if the audience knows it.}
One number matters here: \nc{}. It sets the classical feature count and the
qubit budget \emph{simultaneously}. That is what makes this apples-to-apples.\\
The quantum side runs first and writes its rows and folds to disk; the classical
side replays them. Nobody picks their own split.\\
The accuracy example is the one to land: 0.890 accuracy, benign F1 exactly zero.
That is why every number today is macro-F1.
}

\begin{frame}{\daytag{31}Feature geometry --- a dataset effect}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{\FIG{fig_geometry.pdf}}

\raggedright
\footnotesize
EMBER's families are $\approx 8\times$ more separable by Fisher ratio, up to

\take{this explains why \emph{every} method does badly on CIC 15-class. It
cannot explain the QSVM-specific failure.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{\daytag{33}Kernel diagnostics --- the answer}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{\FIG{fig_kernel_diagnostics.pdf}}

\raggedright
\footnotesize
First outer training fold, \nc{}~$=6$, IQP encoding, 800 rows. The RBF control
is built on the \textbf{identical} feature matrix --- that is what separates
``these features are hard'' from ``this kernel is bad''.

\take{RBF gives the same spread on both datasets ($0.221$ vs.\ $0.218$). The
fidelity kernel matches it on EMBER ($0.92\times$) and \textbf{collapses to
$\mathbf{0.40\times}$ on CIC}.}
\end{frame}
\note{
\textbf{Day 33 (70s). This is the slide of the week --- slow down.}
The control is the whole design: same features, two kernels.\\
Panel (a), read the orange bars first --- RBF is flat across datasets. So CIC's
features do \emph{not} make a classical kernel concentrate.\\
Then the blue: matches RBF on EMBER, collapses on CIC. That is a property of the
\emph{kernel}.\\
Panel (b): alignment must be read against its floor. A constant kernel scores
$1/\sqrt{15}$ on 15 balanced classes. On CIC the fidelity kernel is
\emph{below} that floor --- indistinguishable from an all-ones matrix.\\
Cost note if asked: 6 minutes, two Gram builds. The sweep-wide concentration
numbers were already logged, so they were free.
}

\begin{frame}{\daytag{33}What this supports --- and what it does not}
\small
\begin{block}{Supported}
The failure on CIC is a property of \textbf{the kernel}, not of the dataset.
The Gram approaches a scaled identity --- every pair looks equally similar ---
and an SVM given that has nothing to separate on. That is what a macro-F1
pinned to chance looks like from the inside.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Not supported}
That tuning \texttt{bandwidth} would repair it. Bandwidth is the identified
mechanism and has never been tuned on CIC beyond its default --- but no sweep
was run. A hypothesis with a named lever, not a measured fix.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{One tension we state rather than resolve}
Alignment says neither kernel carries label information on CIC, yet classical
SVM reaches $0.16$ there. Alignment is a global unweighted average over all
pairs; an SVM needs no such thing. It ranks dataset difficulty well and predicts
achievable SVM performance poorly.
\end{block}
\end{frame}
\note{
\textbf{Day 33, part 2 (50s). The credibility slide.}
Three blocks, and the second and third are the ones that matter for trust.\\
Do not let anyone leave thinking we fixed CIC. We named a mechanism. Bandwidth
is untested on CIC.\\
The third block is a genuine inconsistency in our own evidence and I would
rather raise it than have a reviewer find it. Alignment is a blunt instrument;
concentration is not, because it describes the matrix the SVM actually receives.
}

\begin{frame}{\daytag{34}Two things I will not overclaim}
\small
\begin{block}{Wilcoxon returned $0.0625$ for all 84 pairs --- that is not a null result}
With 5 paired samples, $0.0625$ is the \emph{smallest attainable} two-sided
$p$-value. The test cannot reach $0.05$ at this fold count, whatever the effect
size. All 84 hitting the floor means every pair achieved the most extreme rank
configuration available. \emph{Fix:} 6 folds puts $0.03125$ in reach --- not a
different test.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{All CIC binary comparisons are untestable}
Every CIC binary run --- classical \textbf{and} quantum --- predates
parent/child run tracking and carries no recoverable sweep boundary. Raw records
show the QSVM at $0.92$--$1.00$, consistent with the earlier ``ties on binary''
reading, but they resolve into two sweeps per \nc{} with no identifier
separating them. \emph{We did not reconstruct a grouping} --- that would invent
a boundary the data does not record.
\end{block}

\take{the limitation is symmetric across frameworks. It is a logging gap, not a
quantum result.}
\end{frame}
\note{
\textbf{Day 34, part 2 (55s). Expect questions here.}
The Wilcoxon point is the one people get wrong: 0.0625 looks like failure and is
actually the floor. If we reported ``Wilcoxon found nothing'' that would be a
serious misreading of our own data.\\
CIC binary is the awkward one --- it is the only place quantum was ever
competitive, and it is exactly the place we cannot test. I would rather say that
than quietly drop the configuration.\\
If pressed on why not group by timestamp: because the gaps are not a recorded
boundary, they are a guess, and a guess here would fabricate a result.
}

\vspace{0.4em}
\begin{block}{The transferable result}
The fidelity kernel does not fail on CIC because CIC's features are hard --- a
classical RBF kernel on identical features does not concentrate. It fails
because \textbf{the fidelity kernel collapses to $\mathbf{0.40\times}$ the
classical kernel's spread}. That is a statement about the method, not the
dataset.
\end{block}
